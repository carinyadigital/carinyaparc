There is a version of this story where we bought a farm because we love food. It is a nice version and it is roughly true. But the order in which things actually happened is worth getting right, because the order is the lesson.

We did not start with cattle, or trees, or a grand syntropic design. We started with six raised beds outside the kitchen door, because we needed something to cook with and the nearest good greengrocer is 45 minutes away.

## Six beds, one autumn

The beds went in during our first autumn, built from hardwood sleepers left over from the previous owners' yards. Two metres by one, five of them plus one long one for herbs. We filled them with what we could get: a trailer of bought garden mix, a lot of the old alpaca manure that had been composting in a heap behind the shed for who knows how long, and whatever leaves we could rake.

Then we planted like people who had cooked professionally and gardened never. Too much of everything, too close together.

The first season was chaotic and mostly delicious. Silverbeet that would not stop. Coriander that bolted in a week. Radishes, because radishes forgive anyone. And a bed of tomatoes that produced the fruit that started all of this.

## The ordinary tomato

It was a Tommy Toe. Nothing rare. The seedling came from a punnet at the Stroud hardware.

But the first one we ate off that vine, warm, split slightly, tasted like the tomatoes we had spent years chasing through restaurant suppliers and farmers' markets. Sharp, sweet, that green stem smell on your fingers. We stood in the garden and ate about six of them and did not say much.

Here is what we have worked out since, and it is the thing that made us take the rest of the farm seriously. That tomato had nothing to do with us being good gardeners. We were not. It had to do with the soil in that bed, which was mostly ten-year-old alpaca manure, and the fact that we picked it ripe and ate it within a minute.

Flavour is soil and time. Everything else is technique.

## What the kitchen taught the farm

We have a habit, from the cooking years, of tasting everything. So we started tasting the garden the way you would taste a sauce. Two beds of the same lettuce, one with the old manure and one with bought mix. The manure bed won, every time, and not subtly. Carrots from the bed we forgot to water were sweeter than the ones we fussed over.

None of this is a controlled experiment. We are two people with a notebook and a habit. But it changed what we were measuring on the rest of the property. When we pulled soil cores in spring, the question in our heads was not "how is the carbon?" It was "what will this grow, and what will it taste like?"

It also set the standard for the cattle. The point of Highland beef on this pasture is not a breed or a label. It is that the pasture is the flavour, and the pasture is the soil. If the paddocks are not right, the beef will not be, and no amount of dry-ageing will hide it.

## The beds, two years on

We are up to eleven beds now, and about half of the soil in them is our own compost rather than bought. The chaos is slightly more organised. We still plant too much coriander.

This autumn is going in this week: garlic (Italian purple, about 200 cloves), broad beans, broccoli, more silverbeet than any household needs, and a row of the Tommy Toes, because we owe them.

The herb bed is the busiest patch on the property. It is 20 centimetres from the kitchen door and we use it every single day, which is more than we can say for any other part of the 42 hectares yet.

## The point

Start with the thing you will use every day. For us that was the kitchen. It gave us produce, it gave us a standard, and it gave us the question that now runs the whole place: what does this soil taste like?

We will keep the recipes coming from those beds. They will be simple, because the ingredients are doing the work. That was always the best kind of cooking anyway.
