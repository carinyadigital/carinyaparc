## Green grass in winter

Visitors to The Branch in July sometimes assume the vivid green paddocks mean spring has arrived early. On the mid-north coast, lush pasture in midwinter is normal. Cool nights, reliable moisture, and slower evaporation keep grasses active while growth rates stay moderate.

We are learning to read that green as a seasonal signal, not a promise that the land is ready for heavy grazing pressure.

## Recovery still matters

Even when pasture looks full, the underlying system is still building. We are in an establishing phase — rehabilitating exhausted country, improving soil structure, and planning for stock that are not yet on the property at scale.

That means our pasture decisions are as much about **rest** as about **use**:

- Observing which areas hold moisture and which drain quickly after rain.
- Noting where native regeneration is competing well with introduced grasses.
- Leaving buffer zones along riparian areas we are restoring, even when adjacent pasture looks underutilised.

## What we do not claim

We are not running a commercial grazing operation yet. We will not pretend our paddocks are optimised or that we have solved the balance between production and regeneration.

What we can share is the practice of showing up — walking the same routes, photographing the same slopes through seasons, and building a baseline so future decisions are grounded in observation.

## Connecting soil and plate

Our culinary background still shapes how we see pasture. Flavour starts in soil health. The link between what grows here and what might eventually reach a kitchen is long, but it is the reason we care about recovery periods and honest carrying capacity.

## Looking toward spring

Winter pasture work for us is mostly observation and light intervention — fencing access, marking wet areas, planning where summer rest might happen. When spring wildflowers and active growth return, we want a record of what midwinter looked like, not a memory filtered by optimism.

If you are stewarding land in a similar climate, we would rather compare notes than offer prescriptions. Every farm earns its own rhythm.
